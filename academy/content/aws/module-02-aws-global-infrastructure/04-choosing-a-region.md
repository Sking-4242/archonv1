# How to Choose a Region

## Overview

Region selection is one of the first decisions you make when starting an AWS architecture project, and unlike many configuration choices that can be changed later, it has lasting implications that are expensive to reverse. Moving a workload from one Region to another means migrating data, reconfiguring networking, re-validating compliance, and potentially facing service interruptions — none of which are trivial. Getting the Region choice right upfront is not academic; it directly affects your compliance standing, your users' experience, what services you can use, and how much you pay every month.

The decision is not arbitrary, and it is not a matter of personal preference. There is a structured framework with four factors, listed in a strict priority order: Compliance and data residency first, Proximity to users second, Service availability third, and Pricing last. These factors are ordered by how difficult they are to override. Legal requirements cannot be negotiated away with a better latency number. Latency cannot be fixed by choosing a cheaper Region. Service availability is a hard constraint — if a service does not exist in your Region, you cannot use it there. Pricing is real but it is the most flexible factor; a few percentage points of cost difference rarely justifies accepting a compliance risk or degrading user experience.

This lesson exists because the Cloud Practitioner exam directly tests this framework, and because students who learn it as a checklist rather than a reasoning model tend to misapply it on scenario questions. The exam presents situations where one factor overrides the others, and it tests whether you can identify which factor wins. More importantly, this framework is what you will actually use when an architect or engineering manager asks you to recommend a Region for a new project. Walking through the four factors in order — and being able to explain why you skipped or applied each one — is a skill you will use throughout a cloud career.

## Core Concepts

### Factor 1: Compliance and Data Residency

Compliance is always the first filter applied to Region selection, and it operates differently from the other three factors: it is binary. Either a Region meets your compliance requirements, or it does not. There is no partial compliance. No amount of latency optimization, service availability, or cost savings can substitute for a mandatory legal requirement.

Data residency laws are regulations that require certain types of data to be stored and processed within a specific national or geographic boundary. The most well-known is GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) in the European Union, which does not mandate EU-only storage by itself but creates significant barriers to transferring personal data outside the EU. Other examples: Russia's Federal Law 242-FZ requires personal data of Russian citizens to be stored on servers physically located in Russia. India's data localization regulations (evolving as of 2024) impose similar requirements for certain categories of data. China's Cybersecurity Law requires that data generated by Chinese citizens be stored within China. For some of these, AWS has Regions that satisfy the requirement (eu-central-1 for Germany, ap-northeast-1 for Japan, cn-north-1 and cn-northwest-1 for China, which are operated by a separate Chinese entity). For others, no AWS Region may satisfy the requirement, meaning cloud-only architectures may not be viable.

Industry-specific regulations add another layer. HIPAA (US healthcare data) does not mandate a specific Region but requires a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with AWS and appropriate configuration. PCI-DSS (payment card data) similarly imposes configuration requirements without mandating a specific Region, but third-party assessors may have views on which Regions are acceptable. FedRAMP High (US government workloads) limits you to AWS GovCloud Regions (us-gov-east-1 and us-gov-west-1).

The practical implication: before any other factor is evaluated, ask your legal and compliance team whether there are geographic constraints on where the data can live. If the answer is yes, the geographic constraint defines your candidate Region set, and the remaining three factors are applied only within that set.

### Factor 2: Proximity to Users

After compliance has defined which Regions are legally permissible, the next filter is latency — how close is the Region to the users who will interact with the system? Physical distance determines baseline latency. Light travels through fiber at roughly two-thirds the speed of light in a vacuum, so a trans-Atlantic round-trip (about 7,000 km) takes a minimum of 70ms just for the physics — real-world routing typically adds 80–120ms. A trans-Pacific round-trip (roughly 10,000 km) can add 150–200ms. For an interactive web application, those numbers are noticeable.

The right question is not "which Region is geographically closest to my headquarters?" It is "which Region is geographically closest to the largest concentration of my users?" These are often different. A US company with 80% of its users in Western Europe should seriously consider eu-west-1 (Ireland) or eu-west-3 (Paris), not us-east-1 (N. Virginia), even if the engineering team sits in New York.

The best way to measure latency rather than guessing it is to use cloudping.info — a community-maintained tool that measures real-world HTTP latency from your browser to various AWS Regions. Run the test from locations representative of where your users will be connecting from (you can ask users to run it, or use a VPN to simulate other locations). The measurements account for real routing paths, not just physical distance, which can sometimes make a geographically closer Region perform worse than expected due to internet routing inefficiencies.

For global user bases where no single Region is close to everyone, the solution is multi-Region deployment with Route 53 latency-based routing. Each user request is routed to the Region with the lowest measured latency from the user's location. This is more complex and more expensive than single-Region deployment, so the decision requires modeling the latency impact on user experience against the additional operational and cost burden.

### Factor 3: Service Availability

AWS does not offer the same set of services in every Region. Core services — EC2, S3, VPC, RDS, IAM — are available in virtually every Region. But specialized services — particularly newer AI/ML services, industry-specific offerings, and services in early availability — may be limited to a handful of Regions.

Before committing to a Region, enumerate every AWS service your architecture requires and verify availability at the AWS Regional Services List: `aws.amazon.com/about-aws/global-infrastructure/regional-product-services/`. This page is organized by service category and shows a grid of Regions and which services are available in each. Bookmark it. You will use it regularly.

Services tend to expand their regional footprint over time, but "over time" can mean months to years. Launching in a Region missing a required service forces an architectural compromise: either redesign around the missing service, run a hybrid architecture where some components live in a different Region (adding complexity and data transfer costs), or wait until the service launches in your Region of choice.

For the exam, the important patterns are: us-east-1 is almost always the first Region to get new services and has the broadest catalog. eu-west-1 (Ireland) and ap-southeast-1 (Singapore) are typically among the first outside the US to receive service expansions. Newer Regions — particularly in Africa, the Middle East, and some Asia-Pacific areas — often have a more limited catalog.

### Factor 4: Pricing

AWS pricing for the same resource type varies by Region, typically within a range of 10–30%. us-east-1 (N. Virginia) is the cheapest Region for most services, reflecting the scale and maturity of that Region. European and Asia-Pacific Regions are typically 10–15% more expensive due to higher local costs — power, real estate, regulatory compliance overhead, and staffing. Regions in South America and Africa tend to carry higher premiums still.

For many organizations, pricing is the wrong thing to optimize first. A 15% Regional cost difference on a $10,000/month AWS bill is $1,500/month — meaningful, but rarely worth accepting a compliance risk or significantly degrading user experience. However, for cost-sensitive workloads where compliance and latency requirements do not constrain Region choice — batch processing, internal tools, development environments — the pricing factor can meaningfully differentiate candidates.

The right way to compare pricing across Regions is the AWS Pricing Calculator at `calculator.aws`. Build your architecture in the calculator for your primary Region candidate, then duplicate the estimate and change the Region to compare. Pay attention not just to instance and service costs but to data transfer costs. Transferring data between Regions is always charged — the sending Region charges per-GB for cross-Region data transfer out, and these costs can dwarf service costs for data-intensive architectures.

## Configuration Reference

### Checking Service Availability by Region

**Method 1: AWS Regional Services List (Web)**

Go to `aws.amazon.com/about-aws/global-infrastructure/regional-product-services/` in your browser. The page displays a filterable table with services as rows and Regions as columns. A checkmark (or colored indicator) shows whether a service is available in that Region. Use the filter bar to search for a specific service by name.

For example, to verify Amazon Bedrock availability before choosing a Region for an AI workload:
1. Open the Regional Services List.
2. Use Ctrl+F (browser find) or the page's filter to search for "Bedrock."
3. Scan across the Regions — you will see Bedrock is available in us-east-1, us-west-2, eu-central-1, ap-northeast-1, and a growing number of others, but not every Region.

**Method 2: AWS Console Service Discovery**

When you are in the AWS Console and switch Regions (using the top-right Region selector), services that are not available in the selected Region typically either disappear from the navigation or display a notice that the service is not available in the current Region. This is a quick in-context check, though the Regional Services List is more systematic.

**Method 3: AWS CLI — Describe Available Services**

For some service APIs, you can check regional endpoints programmatically. For EC2 (which is available everywhere), verify a Region is enabled:

```bash
aws ec2 describe-regions \
  --region-names us-east-1 eu-central-1 ap-southeast-1 \
  --output table
```

Breaking down this command:
- `aws ec2 describe-regions` — lists Region metadata from the EC2 API.
- `--region-names` — filters the output to specific Regions you are evaluating, rather than returning all Regions. Comma-separate multiple Region codes.
- `--output table` — human-readable format for quick visual comparison.

For checking whether a specific service is available in a Region, there is no universal AWS CLI command — you have to consult the Regional Services List or attempt an API call to the service endpoint and observe whether it returns a valid response or a "service not supported in this region" error.

### Comparing Pricing Across Regions — AWS Pricing Calculator

1. Navigate to `calculator.aws` in your browser.
2. Click **"Create estimate"**.
3. In the search bar, find the service you want to price (e.g., "EC2").
4. Click **"Configure"** on the service. On the configuration screen, look for the **"Region"** dropdown at the top — this is where you choose which Region's pricing applies.
5. Configure your instance type, usage hours, storage, and data transfer assumptions. Click **"Add to estimate"**.
6. In the estimate summary, you can duplicate the line item (click the three-dot menu → "Copy estimate") and change the Region in the copy to compare prices side by side.
7. Review the monthly estimate total for each Region candidate. Note that data transfer costs are often under-estimated — add a realistic data transfer out volume based on your expected traffic patterns.

**Key pricing differences to be aware of:**
- EC2 on-demand pricing in us-east-1 vs. eu-central-1 (Frankfurt): Frankfurt is typically 10–15% higher for equivalent instance types.
- Data transfer out to the internet: pricing is per GB and varies by Region — check the EC2 pricing page for your Region at `aws.amazon.com/ec2/pricing/on-demand/` under "Data Transfer."
- Data transfer between Regions: charged at the sending Region's inter-Region transfer rate (typically $0.02–$0.09/GB depending on source and destination). This cost adds up quickly for data-intensive architectures.

### Console Navigation: Switching Regions to Verify Resources

When verifying that resources exist or that a service is available in a specific Region:

1. Log in to the AWS Console at `console.aws.amazon.com`.
2. Click the Region name in the top-right corner (e.g., "N. Virginia") to open the Region selector.
3. Click the Region you want to evaluate.
4. The console reloads showing resources in the new Region. Navigate to the service in question (via the top search bar or the Services menu).
5. If the service is not available in the selected Region, the console will either hide the service from navigation or display a banner: "This service is not available in [Region name]."

## How to Decide

Apply the four factors in strict order. Stop at the first factor that eliminates all but one candidate.

| Step | Factor | Action | When It Decides |
|------|--------|--------|-----------------|
| **1** | **Compliance / Data Residency** | Consult legal/compliance team. Identify any geographic constraints on data storage or processing. | If constraints exist, your candidate Regions are only those that satisfy them. Often this narrows to 1–3 options immediately. |
| **2** | **Proximity to Users** | Identify where the majority of users are located. Use cloudping.info to measure latency from user locations to candidate Regions. | Choose the Region with the lowest latency to the largest user concentration. If users are global, model multi-Region. |
| **3** | **Service Availability** | List every AWS service the architecture requires. Check each against the AWS Regional Services List for each candidate Region. | If a required service is missing from your leading candidate, either pick a different Region or redesign the architecture. |
| **4** | **Pricing** | Use the AWS Pricing Calculator to estimate monthly cost for each remaining candidate Region, including data transfer costs. | Choose the lower-cost Region when other factors do not differentiate between candidates. |

**Scenario quick-reference:**

| Scenario | Region to Consider First | Why |
|----------|-------------------------|-----|
| US startup, US users, no compliance constraints | us-east-1 | Broadest service catalog, lowest pricing, well-documented |
| European company, GDPR applies, EU users | eu-central-1 or eu-west-1 | EU data residency; Frankfurt for German users, Ireland for broader EU |
| UK company, post-Brexit UK data residency required | eu-west-2 (London) | UK data must stay on UK soil; Ireland does not qualify post-Brexit |
| Japanese users, no compliance constraints | ap-northeast-1 (Tokyo) | Closest AWS Region to Japan |
| US federal government workload (FedRAMP High) | us-gov-west-1 or us-gov-east-1 | Only GovCloud Regions are FedRAMP High authorized |
| Architecture requires a service only in one Region | That Region (then verify compliance/latency) | Service availability overrides latency and pricing once compliance is satisfied |
| Cost-sensitive batch processing, no user interaction | Compare us-east-1 vs. us-west-2 | No user latency concern; optimize for cost; both have broad service catalogs |

## How This Connects

- **AWS Regions** (covered in the first lesson of this module) are the geographic units this decision is selecting between. Every concept in that lesson — independence, service availability variation, us-east-1 as the hub — directly informs the Region selection decision.
- **Availability Zones** within the chosen Region are the next layer of design decisions. Once you have chosen a Region, you need to decide how many AZs to span for redundancy. Region selection and AZ strategy are sequential decisions, not simultaneous ones.
- **Amazon CloudFront** provides an architectural workaround for the latency factor. If compliance mandates a Region that is far from your users, CloudFront Edge Locations can cache content globally and reduce the latency users experience on static assets, even if the origin Region is geographically distant. This does not solve the latency problem for dynamic, non-cacheable requests, but it meaningfully reduces it for content-heavy applications.
- **AWS Pricing Calculator** (`calculator.aws`) is the correct tool for the pricing factor. Cost Explorer shows historical costs for running workloads; the Pricing Calculator is for estimating future costs before deployment. These are different tools for different purposes, and the exam has tested this distinction.
- **IAM and AWS Organizations** are global services unaffected by Region selection — your IAM policies, service control policies (SCPs), and account structure apply uniformly across all Regions. Region selection does not require re-doing any IAM work.

## Exam Traps

- **Students often think compliance is just one of four equal factors, but it is an absolute filter that overrides all others.** If the law says data must stay in Germany, you use eu-central-1. No latency argument, pricing advantage, or service catalog breadth in another Region is relevant once a legal constraint applies.
- **Students often confuse "closest to my team" with "closest to my users" when applying the latency factor, but these are often different locations.** The latency that matters is user-to-Region latency, not engineer-to-Region latency. Engineers can accept higher latency to a development endpoint; end users cannot be asked to accept a worse experience.
- **Students often think the AWS Pricing Calculator shows historical spend, but it is a forward-looking estimator.** Historical and current spend analysis is done through AWS Cost Explorer. The Pricing Calculator is for modeling what a proposed architecture would cost, before you deploy it. These are different tools.
- **Students often think us-east-1 is always the best default choice, but there are specific cases where it is clearly wrong.** Any European compliance requirement, any Japanese user base, any US government FedRAMP requirement — in each of these cases, us-east-1 is the wrong answer regardless of its pricing or service breadth advantages.
- **Students often overlook data transfer costs when comparing Regions on pricing, but inter-Region data transfer can dominate total cost for data-heavy architectures.** A Region that saves 10% on EC2 costs but requires frequent cross-Region data transfers to reach your users or other services may be more expensive in total than a Region with higher compute pricing and no cross-Region transfers.

## Summary

- Region selection follows a strict four-factor priority: Compliance first (legal requirements are non-negotiable), Proximity to users second (latency affects every user interaction), Service availability third (missing services force architectural compromises), and Pricing last (a real but rarely dominant factor).
- Compliance and data residency requirements are determined by consultation with legal teams, not by architects alone — regulations like GDPR, HIPAA, FedRAMP, and national data localization laws define permissible geographies, and non-compliance carries legal and financial consequences.
- Use cloudping.info to measure actual latency from user locations to candidate AWS Regions before making a latency-based Region decision; physical proximity does not always translate to lowest latency due to internet routing.
- The AWS Regional Services List at `aws.amazon.com/about-aws/global-infrastructure/regional-product-services/` is the authoritative source for verifying which services are available in which Regions — check it before committing to a Region for any new project.
- The AWS Pricing Calculator at `calculator.aws` is the correct tool for estimating future architecture costs; AWS Cost Explorer is for analyzing historical spend. Do not confuse them.
- Document your Region selection rationale explicitly — which factors applied, what you checked, and what trade-offs you accepted — because the decision has compliance, architectural, and financial implications that must be traceable.

## Examples

**Beginner:** A US-based startup building a consumer mobile app for North American users applied the framework and made it through in about five minutes. Compliance: no data residency requirements for their use case (social app, no healthcare or financial data). Latency: users are in the US, so an eastern US Region is appropriate. Service availability: their stack uses EC2, RDS, S3, and Lambda — all universally available. Pricing: us-east-1 is the cheapest. Decision: us-east-1. This is the easy case, and the framework's value here is mostly to confirm the default rather than to surface a non-obvious choice. The framework's power becomes visible when you contrast it against harder decisions.

**Intermediate:** A healthcare software company building a patient records platform for the UK National Health Service navigated both GDPR (technically an EU framework, but the UK implemented its own equivalent post-Brexit as UK GDPR) and NHS data governance policies, which together required patient data to remain on UK soil. This immediately eliminated eu-west-1 (Ireland), eu-central-1 (Frankfurt), and every non-UK Region. The only qualifying Region was eu-west-2 (London). eu-west-2 is more expensive than eu-west-1 — roughly 8–10% for equivalent EC2 resources — and it had a slightly narrower service catalog at the time. Neither factor mattered, because compliance set the constraint that eliminated all other options. The team documented this reasoning explicitly in their Architecture Decision Record, which became useful six months later when a finance team member questioned why they were not using a cheaper Region.

**Advanced:** A machine learning platform company designed a pipeline combining Amazon SageMaker for training, AWS Trainium instances for cost-efficient inference, and Amazon Bedrock for foundation model access. They wanted to serve customers in Southeast Asia with low latency. However, at the time of their architecture review, no Region in Southeast Asia offered all three services simultaneously — ap-southeast-1 had SageMaker and Bedrock but not Trainium; the Regions with Trainium were primarily us-east-1 and us-west-2. They applied the framework: compliance was not a constraint. Latency favored ap-southeast-1. Service availability was the deciding factor that overrode latency. Their final architecture placed training and inference workloads in us-east-1 (where all services were available) and used CloudFront to reduce latency on the API delivery layer for Southeast Asian customers. Their API call latency for non-ML content dropped to acceptable levels; the ML inference calls carried the cross-Pacific latency, which they mitigated by pre-computing and caching common inference results at the edge. The lesson: when service availability forces you to a Region that does not optimize for latency, a layered architecture with edge delivery can compensate for some — though not all — of the latency impact.

## Think About It

1. The framework ranks Compliance first, then Latency, then Services, then Pricing. Can you construct a realistic scenario where reordering these factors would actually be the right call? What would that situation look like, and is it truly an exception to the framework or a variant of it?
2. A company has users evenly distributed across the US, Europe, and Southeast Asia. They are considering a single-Region deployment in us-east-1 to keep operational complexity low. What specific latency and availability trade-offs should they model before dismissing a multi-Region approach? At what point does user experience degradation justify the operational overhead?
3. If a service you want to use is only available in us-east-1, but your compliance requirement mandates eu-central-1, what are your architectural options? Which of those options are genuinely acceptable, and which merely avoid the problem while carrying hidden risks?
4. Pricing varies across Regions by 10–30%. When is a 15% cost difference actually worth weighing heavily in Region selection, and when is it noise compared to the other factors? What characteristics of the workload would change your answer?
5. AWS documentation advises consulting legal and compliance teams before finalizing a Region. Why cannot an architect make this call independently, even one who has read all of the relevant compliance documentation? What is the compliance team providing that the documentation does not?

## Quick Check

**Q1.** In the Region selection framework, what is the correct priority order of the four factors?

- A) Pricing → Latency → Compliance → Services
- B) Latency → Services → Compliance → Pricing
- C) Compliance → Latency → Services → Pricing
- D) Services → Compliance → Pricing → Latency

**Answer: C** — Compliance is always evaluated first because legal requirements are non-negotiable and override all other considerations. After compliance, optimize for latency, then verify service availability, then compare pricing among the remaining candidates.

---

**Q2.** A company needs to serve users in Tokyo with minimal latency and has no compliance or data residency constraints. Which Region should they evaluate first?

- A) us-east-1 (N. Virginia) — it has the broadest service catalog
- B) ap-southeast-1 (Singapore) — it is the most popular Asia-Pacific Region
- C) ap-northeast-1 (Tokyo) — it is the AWS Region in Japan
- D) eu-central-1 (Frankfurt) — it has the lowest pricing

**Answer: C** — With no compliance constraints, latency becomes the deciding factor. ap-northeast-1 (Tokyo) is the AWS Region physically located in Japan and is geographically closest to users in Japan, making it the correct first evaluation for a Tokyo user base.

---

**Q3.** Which tool should you use to estimate the future monthly cost of an AWS architecture before deploying it, and which tool shows you what you have already spent?

- A) AWS Cost Explorer for estimates; AWS Pricing Calculator for historical spend
- B) AWS Pricing Calculator for estimates; AWS Cost Explorer for historical spend
- C) AWS Budgets for estimates; AWS Trusted Advisor for historical spend
- D) AWS Config for estimates; AWS CloudTrail for historical spend

**Answer: B** — The AWS Pricing Calculator (`calculator.aws`) is the forward-looking estimation tool: you configure your intended architecture and it projects monthly costs. AWS Cost Explorer analyzes historical billing data for workloads that have already run. The exam tests this distinction because the two tools are often confused.
